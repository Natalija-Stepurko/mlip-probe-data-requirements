Choosing a train/test regime when data is limited

A probe trained on a few hundred materials gets a score. That score carries two errors that are usually conflated. The shortfall is how far it sits below what the same probe reaches on more data; it is set by training size and always points down. The spread is how far it moves when the split is redrawn; it is set by test size. This page measures the two separately, on frozen ORB-v3 and UMA-S embeddings over nine Materials Project properties, with composition-only Magpie features run on the same draws as a baseline, and uses the result to decide how much data is enough, where to put the split, whether cross-validation is worth it, and from how many materials an embedding's score says more than the formula.

The score and the spread are both read off the measured curves. The total error combines them as √(shortfall² + sd²). The bar splits that total by squared contribution. One adjustment goes into the total: the spread was measured on a probe trained on 20,000 materials (6,427 for bulk modulus). A probe trained on less sits further from its ceiling and scatters more, so the spread is scaled up by (1−score)/(1−ceiling) first. This treats the 20,000-material probe as if it were at its ceiling, so the scaled spread errs high.

What this page measures.

  • The size of each error, in R² or macro-F1, for frozen ORB-v3 and UMA-S embeddings on nine Materials Project targets: formation energy, energy above hull, band gap, density, magnetic density, bulk modulus, crystal system, metal / insulator and magnetic / not.
  • Whether cross-validation's advantage survives measurement at the few hundred to few thousand materials an experimental dataset actually has.
  • Whether the answer depends on which model produced the embedding.
  • How much of each score composition alone accounts for, and from how many materials the embedding's lead over it is resolvable.

Why two models. Shortfall varies five- to ninefold across targets at a fixed training size, so a threshold derived from one property would be wrong for the next, and one derived from one model might be wrong for another. ORB-v3 and UMA-S are architecturally opposite: a non-equivariant force field, and an equivariant multi-task model with a spherical-harmonic backbone. Agreement between them makes the thresholds a property of the task.

Why a composition baseline. Magpie (Ward et al., 2016) turns a formula into 132 statistics of its elements' properties and sees no structure. Run through the same probe on the same draws, it marks how much of each score the formula alone supplies, and the embedding's contribution is the difference.

Where the ceilings come from. Every score on this page is measured against the full-corpus score: what the same probe and embedding reach on an 80/20 split of all 154,875 materials (12,853 for bulk modulus). Those numbers are taken from Stepurko, N. & Laulainen, J. (2026), Out-of-training properties in frozen interatomic potential embeddings are decodable and ordered by physical scale, accepted at the AI4Sci and AI4Mat workshops, NeurIPS 2026. Magpie's ceilings are the same manuscript's composition-baseline control: the same probe on the Magpie features, full 80/20 split.

Known from the literature

  • Test-set size sets the variance of a score; training-set size sets its bias (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, 2009, ch. 7).
  • At around 100 samples a cross-validated score carries error bars of roughly ±10 % (Varoquaux, 2018).
  • Learning curves rise monotonically with training size and rarely saturate at the sizes a study can afford (Cortes et al., 1994; Xu et al., 2023).
  • Cross-validation estimates how well the procedure does on average at that training size, not how well the one model you fitted will do (Bates, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2023).
  • The k fold scores from one cross-validation are not independent — every pair of folds shares most of its training data — so no formula can turn their scatter into a correct error bar in general; any such estimate is biased (Bengio & Grandvalet, 2004). That is why this page measures interval coverage directly.

The design

Everything here is measured on 154,875 Materials Project crystals, each represented by one frozen ORB-v3 or UMA-S embedding vector or by its 132 Magpie composition features, with XGBoost as the probe. The three matrices share one row order, so at every size the frozen test set and each training draw are the same materials for all three arms. Every score is compared against the full-corpus score for that target and feature set, from the manuscript cited at the top of the page.

Nine targets carry the curves. Six regressions: formation energy per atom, energy above hull, band gap, density, magnetic density, and bulk modulus — the last on the 12,853-material elastic subset, the rest on the full corpus. Three classifications: crystal system (seven classes), metal / insulator, and magnetic / not.

Training grid: 50, 100, 200, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 at 30 draws per size, extended to 5,000, 7,000, 10,000 and 20,000 at 10, 7, 7 and 6 draws. Test grid: 20 to 10,000 at 200 draws per size, plus the full 20,000-material test set once. Bulk modulus stops at 5,000 on both axes, with 12, 6, 3 and 1 training draws at 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 5,000.

Two thresholds, because two different things go wrong

The two axes fail differently, and the tables above show it. Too little training data pushes the score down — the median climbs 0.271 → 0.862. The number stays valid, just a lower bound.

Too little test data barely biases the score; it blurs it. The median holds near 0.87 while the worst draw moves by 1.1. So a training shortfall understates your embedding, and a test shortfall means the number simply will not reproduce — which is why they are reported as two numbers, a spread and a shortfall.

The point where a score stabilises and the point where it stops rising are different. Most targets' bands collapse between 100 and 500 training materials; energy above hull needs 2,000. But band gap under XGBoost has a spread below 0.05 from n = 500, where it scores 0.669, and keeps climbing to 0.778 at n = 2,000 against a full-corpus value of 0.885. A score can be perfectly reproducible and still well short of what the embedding can do.

Magpie, the composition-only arm, sits below both embeddings from 100 training materials on six targets. On density it is ahead of both up to 500 training materials and level at 1,000; on bulk modulus the three arms are not separable until about 1,000; on magnetic density it is level with or ahead of UMA-S and ahead of ORB-v3 at every size from 500. How it was measured, step by step

The difficulty is that “which estimator is better” needs a truth to compare against, and on real data the true score is exactly what you do not have.

Make the truth knowable

Set aside 20,000 materials as a frozen reference. A model trained on anything else has a true score: how it does on those 20,000. That is not an estimate — it is the answer every estimator is trying to guess.

Carve datasets that never touch it

Draw a dataset U of n materials from the remaining pool, disjoint from the reference and from every other draw. n runs 100, 200, 500, 1,000, 2,000 — the range a small dataset actually falls in.

Disjointness matters twice. If U overlapped the reference, the truth would be scored partly on materials the model trained on. If draws overlapped each other, 20 repeats would not be 20 independent trials, and the spread between them would come out too narrow.

Run all three estimators on the same U

Single split: 80/20, fit on 0.8n, score the held-out fifth, with a bootstrap interval over it. Repeated holdout: five such splits on different seeds, report the mean, with a t-interval over the five scores. 5-fold CV: five folds over the same U, each fitting on 0.8n and predicting its own fifth; pool all n out-of-fold predictions and score once, with a bootstrap interval over the pooled predictions.

Every arm fits on 0.8n, so whatever downward bias comes from training size is identical across them. All three see the same U, so the comparison is paired and most draw-to-draw noise drops out. What differs is only how the available materials are spent on measuring.

Score each against the same truth

The truth for a draw is the score of a probe fitted on all n materials of U — the one you would deploy — on the reference. All three estimators are scored against it.

The truth costs one extra fit per draw: the probe on all n materials, scored once on the reference. The split and fold models never predict the reference.

Repeat 20 times, and measure both

Accuracy: RMSE of each estimate against its truth, across draws. Coverage: how often the reported 95 % interval actually contains the truth.

Accuracy alone would mislead. An estimator can sit closer on average while claiming a confidence it has not earned, and a number that is usually close but occasionally wrong without warning is worse than one that is honest about its range.

The third criterion — distance from the ceiling — is not measured by this run. It comes from combining these noise figures with the training-size curve earlier on the page, and it was added after the run. It also argues against adopting CV: against repeated holdout, total error changes by a median of under 1 %.

Fix the decision rule before looking

CV is adopted only if its interval covers within [0.90, 0.98] and cuts estimator RMSE by at least 30 % at n ≤ 1,000. Both conditions were written down in advance. The ceiling comparison came later.

Distance from the ceiling is set by training size. An 80/20 split has lower total error than an even one because the even one reduces training data; CV changes total error little because it leaves training size unchanged. Against repeated holdout, every adjustment other than training size acts on the smaller term in 85 of the 90 cells; the exceptions are energy above hull and magnetic density at 100 materials on both embeddings and energy above hull at 200 on UMA-S, where CV cuts total error by 21–59 %.

This treats distance from the ceiling as error, which corresponds to reading a small-n score as a floor on the embedding's capability. Measured against the score achievable with the data in hand, the shortfall from the ceiling drops out, and what remains is the estimator RMSE and coverage above: CV is closer in 84 % of cells, by a median 16.8 % at n ≤ 1,000, and its interval covers 0.808 on average.

what this does not settle
  • Every target runs at 20 draws except bulk modulus, whose pool supplies 12, 6 and 3 at 500, 1,000 and 2,000 materials, so its per-cell coverage is noisier.
  • The comparison uses a frozen 20,000-material reference (6,426 on bulk modulus) and stops at 2,000 materials per dataset; larger datasets are not measured.
  • Cost is not the argument. Five folds is five fits, the same as five repeated splits. Cross-validation is rejected on its coverage and on the size of what it buys.

Conclusion

Three properties of a score follow from the split sizes, and the first two are independent:

propertythresholdmeasured from
spread — how far a re-split moves the scoreset by n_testp10–p90 is 0.081 at 200 test materials, 0.033 at 1,000
shortfall — how far the score sits below the full corpusset by n_train0.061 at 5,000 training materials; targets range 0.017 to 0.128 there
gain over composition — what the embedding adds to the formulaset by n_train and the targetresolvable from 500 training materials or fewer on 6 targets, from 2,000 on density, never on magnetic density

At a given dataset size the first two trade off against each other through the split, and neither is fixed by the dataset size alone — both are set by where the split is put. A score from 5,000 training and 200 test materials reproduces to about ±0.040 and sits about 0.061 below the full-corpus value. Both hold simultaneously: the number moves by that much on a re-split and it is a lower bound.

The second one has to be reported per target because it does not summarise. At 5,000 training materials formation energy is 0.017 short and crystal system 0.128, and the span widens with training data. No single number of materials stands in for it: the shortfall differs about five- to ninefold between the easiest and hardest target at every size.

The third does not follow from the other two. Whether the embedding beats the formula alone is resolvable from 500 training materials or fewer on 6 targets, from 2,000 on density, and never on magnetic density, where composition alone is ahead of ORB-v3 and, from 2,000 to 10,000 training materials, of UMA-S. A score can be settled and near its ceiling and still owe everything to the formula, so the gain over composition has to be reported beside the shortfall, per target, to say whether it does.

The regime that keeps total error within 0.005 of the minimum at every size from 500 materials up is an 80/20 split, repeated five times, with no cross-validation. The t-interval over those five scores contains the truth in 0.851 of draws on average against a nominal 0.95, so its width understates the uncertainty. The best fraction drifts toward training data as the dataset grows — 80/20 has the lowest median total error at 500 and 5,000 materials, and 90/10 edges it by 0.001–0.004 at the other sizes from 1,000 up — but from 1,000 materials up 90/10, 80/20 and 70/30 sit within 0.01 of each other, so an 80/20 split, with a wider test fraction when a dataset is small, covers the measured range.

The floor is 5,000 training and 200 test materials — at 80/20 the training floor takes 6,250 materials in total, which puts 1,250 in the test set. No size between 5,000 and 20,000 is a natural cut, so the floor is set by how much data it is reasonable to demand.
