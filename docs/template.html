Three questions behind every small-data benchmark

How much data do you need before a comparison between materials representations can be trusted? A score from a small dataset can mislead in three separate ways, and this page measures each one on frozen ORB-v3 and UMA-S embeddings over nine Materials Project properties.

  1. Is the model trained on enough materials? A probe trained on few materials scores below what the same probe reaches with more data. That gap is the shortfall, and training size sets it.
  2. Would another test set give the same score? A small test set gives a score that moves when the test set is redrawn. That movement is the spread, and test size sets it.
  3. Does the embedding add anything beyond composition? A good score can come from the chemical formula alone. Composition-only Magpie features, run on the same draws, show how much of each score the embedding adds.

What the measurements show

  • Stability and sufficiency are different. Band gap gives a reproducible score from 500 training materials, but its score keeps rising until 7,000, where it comes within 0.05 of the full-data score.
  • There is no universal minimum dataset size. At 5,000 training materials the shortfall runs from 0.017 on formation energy to 0.128 on crystal system.
  • A strong representation can lose to composition when data is scarce. Composition alone is ahead of both embeddings on density up to 500 training materials, and neither embedding resolvably beats it on magnetic density.
  • The limiting error switches with dataset size. At 200 materials a 70/30 split is best; from 500 up the shortfall dominates and the best split moves toward training.
  • 80/20 works here for an empirical reason. From 500 materials up it is within 0.004 of the best split at every size measured.
The two errors in one paragraph, and what this page measures

A probe trained on a few hundred materials gets a score. That score carries two errors that are usually conflated. The shortfall is how far it sits below what the same probe reaches on more data; it is set by training size and always points down. The spread is how far it moves when the split is redrawn; it is set by test size. This page measures the two separately, on frozen ORB-v3 and UMA-S embeddings over nine Materials Project properties, with composition-only Magpie features run on the same draws as a baseline, and uses the result to decide how much data is enough, where to put the split, whether cross-validation is worth it, and from how many materials an embedding's score says more than the formula.

What this page measures.

  • The size of each error, in R² or macro-F1, for frozen ORB-v3 and UMA-S embeddings on nine Materials Project targets: formation energy, energy above hull, band gap, density, magnetic density, bulk modulus, crystal system, metal / insulator and magnetic / not.
  • Whether cross-validation's advantage survives measurement at the few hundred to few thousand materials an experimental dataset actually has.
  • Whether the answer depends on which model produced the embedding.
  • How much of each score composition alone accounts for, and from how many materials the embedding's lead over it is resolvable.

Why two models. Shortfall varies five- to ninefold across targets at a fixed training size, so a threshold derived from one property would be wrong for the next, and one derived from one model might be wrong for another. ORB-v3 and UMA-S are architecturally opposite: a non-equivariant force field, and an equivariant multi-task model with a spherical-harmonic backbone. Agreement between them makes the thresholds a property of the task.

Why a composition baseline. Magpie (Ward et al., 2016) turns a formula into 132 statistics of its elements' properties and sees no structure. Run through the same probe on the same draws, it marks how much of each score the formula alone supplies, and the embedding's contribution is the difference.

Where the ceilings come from. Every score on this page is measured against the full-corpus score: what the same probe and embedding reach on an 80/20 split of all 154,875 materials (12,853 for bulk modulus). Those numbers are taken from Stepurko, N. & Laulainen, J. (2026), Out-of-training properties in frozen interatomic potential embeddings are decodable and ordered by physical scale, accepted at the AI4Sci and AI4Mat workshops, NeurIPS 2026. Magpie's ceilings are the same manuscript's composition-baseline control: the same probe on the Magpie features, full 80/20 split.

How it was measured

Everything here is measured on 154,875 Materials Project crystals, each represented by one frozen ORB-v3 or UMA-S embedding vector or by its 132 Magpie composition features, with XGBoost as the probe. The three matrices share one row order, so at every size the frozen test set and each training draw are the same materials for all three arms. Every score is compared against the full-corpus score for that target and feature set, from the manuscript cited at the top of the page.

Nine targets carry the curves. Six regressions: formation energy per atom, energy above hull, band gap, density, magnetic density, and bulk modulus — the last on the 12,853-material elastic subset, the rest on the full corpus. Three classifications: crystal system (seven classes), metal / insulator, and magnetic / not.

Training grid: 50, 100, 200, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 at 30 draws per size, extended to 5,000, 7,000, 10,000 and 20,000 at 10, 7, 7 and 6 draws. Test grid: 20 to 10,000 at 200 draws per size, plus the full 20,000-material test set once. Bulk modulus stops at 5,000 on both axes, with 12, 6, 3 and 1 training draws at 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 5,000.

So a point on the main curves is neither one split nor five: it is many independent training sets, all scored on one large fixed test set. That is what lets the shortfall and the spread be measured separately.

Why so many draws. Each point needs to be precise enough to read a threshold of 0.05 off it. Taking 5 of the 30 draws at each training size up to 2,000, the median lands 0.005 from the 30-draw value on a typical point, and 0.015 or more one time in ten; at 50 training materials, 0.056 or more one time in ten. A spread estimated from 5 draws is off by about a quarter of its value.

Two thresholds, because two different things go wrong

The two axes fail differently, and the tables above show it. Too little training data pushes the score down — the median climbs 0.271 → 0.862. The number stays valid, just a lower bound.

Too little test data barely biases the score; it blurs it. The median holds near 0.87 while the worst draw moves by 1.1. So a training shortfall understates your embedding, and a test shortfall means the number simply will not reproduce — which is why they are reported as two numbers, a spread and a shortfall.

Known from the literature
  • Test-set size sets the variance of a score; training-set size sets its bias (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, 2009, ch. 7).
  • At around 100 samples a cross-validated score carries error bars of roughly ±10 % (Varoquaux, 2018).
  • Learning curves rise monotonically with training size and rarely saturate at the sizes a study can afford (Cortes et al., 1994; Xu et al., 2023).
  • Cross-validation estimates how well the procedure does on average at that training size, not how well the one model you fitted will do (Bates, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2023).
  • The k fold scores from one cross-validation are not independent — every pair of folds shares most of its training data — so no formula can turn their scatter into a correct error bar in general; any such estimate is biased (Bengio & Grandvalet, 2004). That is why this page measures interval coverage directly.

Test size moves the spread and little else. Across the grid the p10–p90 spread falls from 0.239 at 20 test materials to 0.010 at 5,000 — a factor of 24 — while from 200 test materials up the median moves by no more than 0.021. At 200 test materials a re-split moves a typical score by about ±0.040; at 500, ±0.024; at 1,000, ±0.016. This is the axis that decides whether a number reproduces, and the spread at a given test size depends on the property being predicted: at 200 test materials ORB-v3's spread runs from 0.018 on formation energy to 0.189 on magnetic density.

It is not independent of how well the probe scores. Magpie's spread on the regression targets is wider than the embeddings' at every test size up to 500 — on formation energy 0.127 against 0.018 for ORB-v3 at 200 test materials — and on formation energy the ratio tracks 1 − R²: a probe further from a perfect score scatters more. The calculator applies that rescaling before combining spread with shortfall.

Where questions 1 and 2 meet: the split

Total error against the train/test fraction, at six dataset sizes from 200 to 10,000; the full-data tables below add 20,000.

With a fixed number of materials, every split has to decide how many go into the test set, and that choice moves two errors in opposite directions. Fewer materials for training means the probe scores lower than it would with more data: the shortfall, read off the training-size curve. More materials for testing means the score is measured more precisely: the spread, read off the test-size curve. For each split the page reads both off the curves and combines them into one total error; the lowest point on the plot below is the best split for that dataset size.

How the two are combined. The shortfall is a fixed offset: the score sits that far below its ceiling every time. The spread is random: a re-split moves the score up or down. A fixed error and a random one combine as √(shortfall² + sd²), where sd is the random error expressed as a standard deviation. The spread on this page is the width between the 10th and 90th percentile of the score across draws, which for a normal distribution is 2.5631 standard deviations; the page estimates it as exactly that, so sd = spread ÷ 2.5631 recovers the standard deviation. One adjustment comes first: a probe that scores lower scatters more, so the spread is scaled up by (1 − score)/(1 − ceiling), as in the calculator.

Given a small dataset, which way of measuring a probe's score gives the most reliable estimate?

With a small dataset, measuring a probe creates a trade-off. Materials kept back for testing cannot be used for training, but a very small test set gives a noisy score. Three common ways of making that estimate are compared.

methodproceduremodels
trained
single 80/20 splittrain on 80 % of the materials, score on the other 20 %, once1
five 80/20 splits, averagedrepeat five random 80/20 splits and average the five scores5
5-fold cross-validationcut the materials into five equal parts; train on four and predict the fifth, rotating until every material has been predicted once; score all predictions together5

In all three methods, each individual model is trained on 80 % of the small dataset. They differ only in how the held-out materials are used to score it. In 5-fold cross-validation every material is scored exactly once, by a model that was not trained on it.

How the methods were evaluated

Someone who has only a small dataset cannot know how well their probe really performs; that is what the estimate is for. Here the much larger corpus makes it possible to approximate. A frozen test set of 20,000 materials (6,426 for bulk modulus) is kept out of everything. For each small dataset of n materials, a probe is trained on all n and scored on those 20,000. Because that test set is large, this score is used as the reference score for the small dataset. It is a different number from the paper's full-corpus score used elsewhere on this page, which belongs to a probe trained on all 154,875 materials.

Each of the three methods then estimates the score from the n materials alone, and its estimate is compared with the reference score. The comparison covers 90 combinations — two embeddings, nine properties, five dataset sizes from 100 to 2,000 materials — with 20 independent datasets for each (fewer for bulk modulus above 500).

Important caveat. The reference probe is trained on all n materials, while every model inside the three methods is trained on 0.8n. The difference from the reference therefore includes both the error of the method and a small effect of the smaller training set; all three methods share that effect, so it lowers all three estimates slightly.

Main conclusion. For limited datasets, 5-fold cross-validation is the best of the three procedures tested for estimating the score itself, and its advantage is largest when the dataset is very small. Five averaged splits come close; a single split is substantially noisier and should not be used. None of the reported 95 % intervals reaches its stated coverage, cross-validation's least of all, so they should be read with caution, and the comparison carries the training-size mismatch described above.

More on the cross-validation comparison — what it does not change, and its limits

What cross-validation does not change

Cross-validation makes the estimate of a small-data probe's score more accurate. It does not bring that score closer to what the same embedding reaches on the full corpus, because each of its models still trains on 80 % of the data. For an embedding comparison, that distance is usually the larger error.

Band gap, ORB-v3. Bias is the shortfall from the full-corpus score at 80 % of the data; noise is the spread of each method's error across datasets; total combines the two.

At 2,000 materials cross-validation cuts the noise from 0.020 (one split) to 0.011, and the total from 0.125 to 0.124, because the bias of 0.123 dominates.

what this does not settle
  • Each combination rests on 20 datasets, so its own coverage figure is uncertain by about ±5 percentage points; the averages over 90 combinations are firmer.
  • Only the XGBoost probe, only 5 folds, and only datasets of 100 to 2,000 materials were tested.
  • Bulk modulus has fewer datasets above 500 materials (12, 6 and 3 at 500, 1,000 and 2,000) because its pool runs out.

Conclusion

  1. Is the model trained on enough materials? Use a floor of 5,000 training materials. Targets differ five- to ninefold, so for a single property read its own row of the floors table.
  2. Would another test set give the same score? Use a floor of 500 test materials and an 80/20 split; at 80/20 the training floor already brings 1,250 test materials. On small datasets, score with 5-fold cross-validation or five averaged splits, never a single split, and do not quote their 95 % intervals as they stand.
  3. Does the embedding add anything beyond composition? Report the gain over composition per target. The embeddings beat Magpie from 500 training materials or fewer on 6 targets, from 2,000 on density, and never on magnetic density.
